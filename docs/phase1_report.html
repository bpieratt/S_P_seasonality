S&P 500 Seasonality, 2003–2022

I tested a pre-declared grid of calendar windows, plus the 12 calendar months, against 20 years of daily S&P 500 closes. Each was compared against the index's normal drift and corrected for the number of tests run. No market data from 2023 or later was loaded at any step.

Bottom line

The 2003–2022 data does not provide statistically reliable evidence of calendar seasonality in the S&P 500. Of 120 windows, 5 cleared p < 0.05 on a t-test, about the 6 that chance alone would produce. None survive false-discovery-rate correction (best q = 0.64).

In a calendar-scramble simulation that destroys any real seasonality, the best of 120 windows still reached a median |t| of 2.97. The strongest real window, Jul 1 → Jul 2215 td, reached t = 3.15. That result is what the best of 120 random windows typically looks like (family-wise p = 0.40).

Executive summary

  1. Is there meaningful evidence of seasonality? Not statistically. There are recurring tendencies that look interesting, but their number and size match what random noise produces when 120 windows are tested. Classification: 0 Strong, 8 Moderate (nominal only), 32 Weak, 80 None.
  2. Strongest bullish tendencies. Jul 1 → Jul 2215 td: 80% of years up, mean +2.25%, median +2.20%. Mar 16 → Apr 2730 td: 85% up, mean +3.47%. Nov 16 → Dec 715 td: 85% up, mean +1.44%. Apr 16 → Apr 2910 td: 80% up, mean +1.42%.
  3. Strongest bearish tendency and drawdown risk. Sep 16 → Sep 2910 td is the only window with nominal bearish support: 60% of years down, mean -1.21%, median -0.41%. Early June and late January lean weak but are inconclusive. Drawdowns of 3% or more were most frequent in mid-to-late June, late September to mid-October, late February through March, and late January. The deepest of them come from 2008, 2009 and 2020. The drawdown section below separates drawdowns from negative returns.
  4. How often did each occur? The bullish candidates were up in 80–90% of years, against an unconditional base rate of 58–66% for windows of the same length. Late September was down in 60% of years, against a base rate of about 38%.
  5. Average and median moves. These are listed in the tables below. The candidates beat normal drift by roughly 0.6–2.4 percentage points per window. The medians agree in sign with the means, so a single outlier year is not driving any of them.
  6. Consistent across both halves? All 8 Moderate windows have the same sign in 2003–2012 and 2013–2022. The early-July effect is mostly a 2013–2022 phenomenon (100% up, mean +3.14%, vs 60% and +1.36% before). Mid-March–April and mid-November were stronger in 2003–2012.
  7. Which survived statistical testing? None. No window or month survives Benjamini–Hochberg FDR at q ≤ 0.10, and none survives the permutation family-wise test.
  8. Too weak or unstable to rely on. Every calendar month: none has p < 0.05, including the well-known “Sell in May” and “September effect”. Also early June, late January, early October and the Santa-Claus period. The Nov16+30td candidate qualifies only on its Wilcoxon test (t-test p = 0.15).
  9. Economically meaningful? Marginally at best. An excess of +0.6–2.4% over 1–6 weeks is real money if it persists. But the 95% confidence intervals are wide and several nearly touch zero, and the figures exclude costs, slippage, taxes and dividends. Worst-year drawdowns inside the "bullish" windows reached −5% to −17%.
  10. What gets frozen? All 120 windows and 12 months, with exact entry and exit rules and in-sample statistics, go into seasonal_patterns_2003_2022.json. The 8 Moderate windows are flagged as the primary out-of-sample hypotheses. Testing all 132 later prevents cherry-picking after the fact.

Classification rules

The rules were fixed in config.yaml before the results were seen. A window's direction comes from the sign of its mean excess return. "Halves agree" means the mean excess has the same sign in 2003–2012 and 2013–2022. "Robust" means the sign holds after removing the two most favourable years.

Strong
BH-FDR q ≤ 0.10, Wilcoxon p ≤ 0.05, halves agree, robust, and a hit rate of at least 65% in the stated direction. No window qualified.
Moderate*
Raw p ≤ 0.05 on the t-test or Wilcoxon, halves agree, robust. Nominal evidence that does not survive multiplicity correction. 8 windows.
Weak
Raw p ≤ 0.20, or halves agree and robust with a hit rate of at least 60%. 32 windows.
None
Everything else. 80 windows.

Method and assumptions

  • Search grid (not optimised). Windows start on the 1st and the 16th of every month and run for 5, 10, 15, 20 or 30 trading days: 24 anchors × 5 lengths = 120 windows. Start and end dates were never moved to improve results.
  • Entry and exit. Buy at the close of the last trading day before the anchor date. Sell at the close of the Nth trading day. Dec-16 windows of 15 days or more have 19 occurrences, because their 2022 instance would have ended in 2023 and was excluded.
  • Excess return. Each window is tested against the mean return of all overlapping windows of the same length in 2003–2022. This keeps the index's general upward drift from being counted as seasonality.
  • Tests. One-sample t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank on excess return; a binomial sign test against the unconditional win rate; a 95% bootstrap CI (10,000 resamples of years); Cohen's d; Benjamini–Hochberg FDR across the 120 windows (and separately the 12 months); and a 2,000-draw calendar-scramble permutation. The permutation shifts every window in a year by the same random number of days, which preserves window overlap and volatility regimes, and yields max-|t| family-wise p-values.
  • Point-in-time engine. Every trade is evaluated through MarketData.as_of(exit_date). That returns a read-only copy holding only bars on or before that date, so later bars are not in memory. Requesting a later bar raises LookAheadError. The loader raises FutureDataError if any row is dated after 2022-12-31. All 11 guardrail tests pass.
  • Not a trading system. Returns exclude transaction costs, slippage, taxes and dividends (^GSPC is a price index). Window selection is in-sample by construction, so the out-of-sample test is the real evidence.
  • Sample length. Only 2003–2022 was loaded, so there is no longer-history comparison. Twenty observations per window give low statistical power. Effects smaller than about 1.5–2% per window can't be distinguished from noise.
