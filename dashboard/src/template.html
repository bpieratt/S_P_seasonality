Executive summary plain-English answers

A good past record is not the same as a reliable edge. The S&P 500 rose in about two out of every three months in both periods, so almost any calendar window "worked" most of the time. A seasonal pattern only matters if it beats that ordinary upward drift [[i:drift]] by more than luck would explain. None of the 132 calendar rules cleared that bar in the historical data. In the newer data, the bullish rules as a group did no better than randomly chosen dates.
1. Does the S&P 500 show recurring seasonal patterns?
Historical observationNo reliable advantage
There are tendencies, but none is reliable. April rose in 16 of 20 years and July in 15. Yet after allowing for the fact that 120 windows were tested, none stood out from chance. {{p1_raw}} windows looked "significant" on their own, about the {{p1_expected}} that pure luck would produce.
2. Which periods did best and worst historically?
Historical observation
Best months: April ({{apr_avg}} on average), July ({{jul_avg}}) and November ({{nov_avg}}). Weakest: September ({{sep_avg}}), January ({{jan_avg}}) and June ({{jun_avg}}). The most promising shorter windows were mid-March to late April, early-to-late July, mid-April and mid-November to early December. Late September was the one weak window.
3. Did those patterns continue in 2023–2026?
Partly supportedSeveral reversed
Mixed. Two short windows held up in every newer year: Apr 16 → Apr 29 and mid-November. Early July weakened, and the late-September dip did not return. The monthly pattern mostly broke down: January, May and June, historically soft, were among the strongest months of 2023–2026. Only {{month_same_sign}} of 12 months kept their historical direction.
4. What looks encouraging but is still uncertain?
Inconclusive
The Apr 16 → Apr 29 window (up {{apr_oos_n}} of {{apr_oos_n}} newer years, averaging {{apr_oos_mean}}) and the mid-November windows (up 3 of 3). They are consistent with the history, but 3–4 years is far too few to prove anything. Their likely range of outcomes still includes zero or close to it.
5. What does this mean for an investor?
No demonstrated trading edge
The calendar alone has not shown a dependable advantage. In 2023–2026 the bullish windows gained {{q1_pooled}} per occurrence, but random windows of the same length gained nearly as much ({{q1_vs_drift}} difference; placebo p = {{q1_p}}). After trading costs and taxes, an edge that small could disappear. Treat seasonality as background context, not a reason to buy or sell.
How sure are these answers?
Read with care
The historical "no reliable edge" conclusion is solid: it survives every check, including the new sensitivity review. The newer-period findings rest on only 3–4 observations per rule, so treat them as hints worth watching, not conclusions. See Statistical review for what was double-checked.

How to read this heat map
ColorsTeal months rose and red months fell. The darker the color, the bigger the move. Grey means close to flat (between −0.5% and +0.5%). Colors stop getting darker beyond ±8%, but the cell shows the exact number.
Looking for patternsRead down a column to see one calendar month across 20 years. A column that is mostly teal (April, July) suggests a tendency. A column with mixed colors suggests no tendency at all.
Why repetition isn't proofThe market rose in 65% of all months, so most columns are mostly teal by default. With 12 columns, one or two will look streaky by chance. The 2008 and 2020 crashes also dominate the red cells, and those were one-off events, not seasons.

Did the frozen rules hold up? the 8 main hypotheses, unchanged since 2022

These 8 windows were chosen and locked using only 2003–2022 data, then tested on 2023–2026. The pass/fail rules were also written down before the newer data was loaded. Rows stay in the original study's order and are not re-sorted by newer results. Click a row to see every year.

What does this table tell me?
Four windows were "Supportive": they kept at least half their historical edge and went the right way in most newer years. None was contradicted. But the "vs random" column shows how little the calendar added: in the strong 2023–2026 market, randomly timed windows of the same length did nearly as well. Supportive here means "consistent with the history", not "proven".

Statistical review new sensitivity analysis · {{review_date}} · original results unchanged

What this section is.A second look at five places where the original method could have overstated confidence. Each check was run separately (sensitivity/) and reads the frozen research files without changing them. Where a check qualifies an original number, both are shown.
Bottom line of the review
The main conclusion stands, and if anything it is stronger: there is no statistically reliable calendar edge, either in 2003–2022 or in 2023–2026. One original number needs a caveat. The 2023–2026 report quoted p = 0.041 for "historical winners stayed ahead". Once you account for the rules overlapping each other, that becomes p ≈ {{s3_p}}, which is no longer even nominally significant.
